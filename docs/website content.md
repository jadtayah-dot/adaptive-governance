## 2. What the project studies

**Section heading**

Adaptive governance, and when it becomes transformative

**Body**

Adaptive governance is the iterative fine tuning of policy under uncertainty, driven by data and by feedback from implementation. Transformative governance is different in kind: a durable shift in authority, resources or rules. The two are connected. Repeated adaptation can trigger a transformative leap, and a transformative leap often unlocks adaptation that was previously blocked.

This project treats the boundary between them as an empirical question rather than a theoretical one. Each case is coded for adaptive signals, meaning real time feedback loops and revision frequency, and for transformative markers, meaning new mandates, budget reallocations and new institutions. The point is to locate where incremental learning tips into systemic change.

Qatar is a productive place to ask this. It sits at a nexus of local, national, regional and transnational pressure, and its institutions have absorbed technological disruption, climate stress and shifting geopolitical alignment inside a compressed period. The country does not only adapt to these pressures. It also shapes governance discourse across scales, which makes it a case that speaks to global debates rather than a case that only illustrates them.

**Three definitions, displayed as a set**

*Adaptive signals*
Real time feedback loops. Revision frequency. Evidence that a policy instrument is being tuned rather than merely administered.

*Transformative markers*
New mandates. Budget reallocation. New institutions. Evidence that authority or rules have moved, not just settings.

*Adaptive spaces*
The institutional room in which experimentation is permitted, recorded and fed back into decisions.

## 3. The five cases

**Section heading**

Five cases, one comparison

**Intro**

Each case was selected against four criteria: state sponsorship, relevance across more than one scale, accessible data, and alignment with Qatar National Vision 2030. Each is studied through document analysis, structured interviews and participatory methods. A synthesis matrix then compares them, and that comparison is what produces the framework.

**Case one**

*Digital policy labs*
How regulatory experimentation is organised inside Qatar's digital transformation agenda, and what happens to a rule once a lab has tested it.
Led by Dr. Georgios Dimitropoulos and Dr. Kehinde Olaoye, College of Law, with practitioner input from the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology and Ibtechar.

**Case two**

*Smart city platforms*
Urban services as a governance layer: what municipal data changes about who decides, how fast, and on whose evidence.
Led by Dr. Esmat Zaidan, College of Public Policy, with Dr. Sukaina Nasrawi at ESCWA.

**Case three**

*Strategic foresight and national narratives*
How foresight units inside government translate long horizon scenarios into present decisions, and how national narratives constrain what can be planned for.
Led by Dr. Evren Tok, College of Public Policy.

**Case four**

*Crowdsourced sentiment*
Arabic social media data from the FIFA World Cup 2022, analysed to show how daily sentiment reporting fed transport, security and crowd decisions in real time.
Led by Dr. George Mikros, College of Humanities and Social Sciences.

**Case five**

*Humanitarian diplomacy*
Qatar's humanitarian and diplomatic practice as a form of governance that operates across borders, read through the concept of nested power.
Led by Dr. Abdulfatah Mohamed, College of Islamic Studies, with H.E. Dr. Abdulaziz Al Horr at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs.

## 4. Objectives

**Section heading**

What the project is for

**Objective one. Examine adaptive governance in practice.**
Map actor constellations, institutional dynamics and policy innovations across the five cases, then compare them in a synthesis matrix to identify where adaptive and transformative practice actually emerges and whether it can be scaled.

**Objective two. Build tools that make adaptive capacity visible.**
Foresight scenarios, computational linguistic dashboards and an interactive governance dashboard built in Tableau, showing the linkages, feedback loops and actor networks that make up the governance ecosystem. Co designed with ministries and municipalities so that they are usable rather than illustrative.

**Objective three. Build capacity.**
A training course and certificate, two new co taught graduate courses, integration into existing programmes at the College of Public Policy, the College of Law and the College of Humanities and Social Sciences, and supervised student research.

**Objective four. Advance theory and inform reform.**
Peer reviewed case articles, a systematic review, a comparative policy paper and a framework article, benchmarked against global governance debates rather than written only for a local audience.

## 5. The corpus

**Section heading**

The evidence base

**Body**

The project opened with a systematic review of the adaptive governance literature. The search returned 1,172 records. Screening reduced these to 272 that met the inclusion criteria, and full text assessment narrowed that further against a strict definition of adaptive governance as a core concept rather than a passing reference.

The corpus is published here in full. Each record carries its title, authors, year, outlet, a link to the source, and the reason it was kept or excluded. You can filter by country, region, year, theme and method, and search across the set.

It is also what drives the globe on this page. Each country is raised and lit in proportion to the number of studies the corpus holds from it. The distribution is uneven, and that unevenness is a finding: adaptive governance scholarship clusters in a small number of settings, and the Gulf is thin. Part of what this project is doing is making that thinner region legible.

## Style rules for anyone extending this copy

**Hard rules**

1. No hyphens. Not in compounds, not as dashes, not as bullets. Use commas, colons, full stops. Where a compound normally takes one, close it up or split it: multiscalar, co taught, peer reviewed, data driven, smart city, real time.
2. No em dashes and no en dashes. Same reason.
3. Numbers as digits above ten. Dates written out: September 2025, not 09 2025.
4. Titles use Dr., Ms., Mr., H.E. as the person uses them.

**Banned constructions**

Any sentence that could appear on a software company homepage. Specifically avoid: unlock, empower, leverage as a verb, seamless, cutting edge, robust, holistic when it means nothing, journey, ecosystem when it means people, at the intersection of, in today's rapidly changing world, more than ever, we do not just X we Y, the rhetorical question as a heading, the three item list used for rhythm rather than for content, and any sentence beginning with Whether you are.

**Register**

Write as if a reviewer who knows the field is reading. State what the project does and what it has found. Do not sell. If a sentence would survive in a methods section, it will survive here.

**Test**

Read any paragraph aloud. If it sounds like it is trying to make you feel something, cut it. If it sounds like it is telling you something, keep it.
